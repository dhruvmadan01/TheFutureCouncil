# The Launchpad Playbook

A free eight-week path from idea to Demo Day for student founders, by The Future Council.

Read it online: https://thefuturecouncil.in/launchpad/playbook

## How it works

One milestone a week for eight weeks, in pods of four to five founders, then a ranked selection and a Demo Day. Batches start monthly.

## Week 1: Pick a problem

Find one problem worth building a company around.

**Hand in:** A one-page problem statement that names a specific person, the situation they are in, how they cope today and what it costs them.

**Why it matters:** Everything in the next seven weeks is built on the problem you pick now, so spend this week choosing it carefully.

**Watch and read**

- [How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg) (Jared Friedman, Y Combinator). Use the evaluation questions on each of your ten situations.
- [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.htm) (Paul Graham). Short read. Notice his advice to look for problems you have yourself.

**Steps**

1. List ten frustrating situations you have seen or lived in the last month.
2. Score each one against the four tests: specific, frequent, painful, reachable.
3. Pick the strongest one and write the problem statement using the template.
4. Name where you will find ten people who have this problem.

**Problem statement template**

```
Who has this problem:
[A specific kind of person, in a specific situation]

The situation:
[When does it happen, and how often?]

The problem:
[What goes wrong, in plain words?]

How they cope today:
[The workaround, tool or habit they use now]

What it costs them:
[Time, money or stress, with a rough number if you can]

Where I will find 10 of them:
[Specific places, groups or people]
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] Names a specific person or group, not a broad category like 'students' or 'everyone'
- [ ] Describes a recurring situation where the problem happens
- [ ] Describes how people cope with the problem today
- [ ] States what the problem costs them in time, money or stress
- [ ] Says where the founder will find at least 10 people who have this problem

**Common mistakes**

- Describing a solution and calling it a problem, for example 'there is no app for X'.
- Choosing a problem you have never seen anyone else struggle with.
- Describing a group too broadly to be able to find and interview them.

## Week 2: Talk to users

Learn what is true by listening to ten real people.

**Hand in:** Notes from ten real customer conversations and the three insights that matter most.

**Why it matters:** You will learn more from ten honest conversations than from any amount of reading, and the way you ask matters as much as who you ask.

**Watch and read**

- [How To Talk To Users](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z1iF1c8w5Lg) (Gustaf Alströmer, Y Combinator). Write your interview questions after watching.
- [How to Talk to Users](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MT4Ig2uqjTc) (Eric Migicovsky, Y Combinator). A second take on the same skill, from an earlier Startup School.

**Steps**

1. Message at least 20 people from your list so that 10 agree to talk.
2. Run ten conversations using the script. Do not pitch.
3. Write a summary for each conversation within an hour of finishing it.
4. Put all notes in one shared document and write your three insights at the top.

**Conversation notes template**

```
Date and who (role, not just name):

Their situation:

Last time the problem happened:

What they did about it:

What it cost them:

A quote I want to remember:

Strength of signal (1 to 5) and why:

Who else should I talk to:
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] States that at least 10 real conversations took place
- [ ] Notes link is provided and the notes are about the person's past behavior, not opinions about the idea
- [ ] Lists three insights that are supported by what several people said
- [ ] Names something that surprised the founder
- [ ] Does not rely on compliments or hypothetical future use as evidence

**Common mistakes**

- Talking about your idea for most of the call.
- Counting friends who agreed to be polite as validation.
- Taking notes only as opinions, with no stories about what really happened.

## Week 3: Define the solution

Decide the smallest thing that solves the sharpest part of the problem.

**Hand in:** A one-line pitch, the core features, and a clear list of what you will not build.

**Why it matters:** Your first product should be the smallest thing that fixes the sharpest moment from your interviews, and this talk is about exactly that kind of scoping.

**Watch and read**

- [How to Build a "Minimum Viable Product"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QRZ_l7cVzzU) (Michael Seibel, Y Combinator). Use it to write your core features and your not-building list.

**Steps**

1. Reread your week 2 notes and mark the moment that hurt the most.
2. Write the one-line pitch using the template and test it on two people.
3. List three to five core features and a separate list of what you will not build.
4. Write down the single belief that, if wrong, would kill the idea.

**One-line pitch and scope template**

```
One-liner:
For [who] who [problem], [product] is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [what they use today].

Core features (3 to 5):
1.
2.
3.

Not building yet:
-
-
-

Riskiest assumption:
[If this is wrong, the idea does not work]

How I will test it:
[What I will see if it is true]
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] The one-line pitch names who it is for, the problem, and what is different from today's alternative
- [ ] Lists between three and five core features
- [ ] Lists what will deliberately not be built
- [ ] Names a specific riskiest assumption that can be tested
- [ ] The solution clearly follows from the problem and the conversations in week 2

**Common mistakes**

- Listing fifteen features because they all seem useful.
- A one-liner full of words like 'platform', 'ecosystem' or 'AI-powered' with no clear benefit.
- Skipping the not-building list, which is where scope creep begins.

## Week 4: Build a landing page

Put your idea in front of strangers and count who raises a hand.

**Hand in:** A live landing page that collects signups or expressions of interest.

**Why it matters:** A landing page lets you test your idea with strangers in a day, and the point of this week is to launch something small rather than wait until it is ready.

**Watch and read**

- [The Best Way To Launch Your Startup](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u36A-YTxiOw) (Kat Mañalac, Y Combinator). Decide where you will share your page before you build it.

**Steps**

1. Write the headline, one-line explanation, three benefits and one call to action.
2. Build the page and connect the signup form to a sheet you can read.
3. Share it with at least 30 people from the right audience.
4. Record your visit and signup counts and what people said.

**Landing page copy template**

```
Headline:
[Who it is for + what they get]

Subheadline:
[How it works, in one sentence]

Three benefits:
1.
2.
3.

Call to action button:
[One action]

Trust line:
[Who is behind this and why]
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] A live page URL is provided that a stranger can open without logging in
- [ ] The page states who it is for and what they get
- [ ] The page has one clear way to sign up or show interest
- [ ] Reports how many people visited and how many signed up
- [ ] Describes how the page was shared and with whom

**Common mistakes**

- Spending the week on design instead of getting visitors.
- Offering several different calls to action on the same page.
- Counting your own friends' visits and signups as demand.

## Week 5: Ship an MVP

Build something a stranger can use, even if it is rough.

**Hand in:** A working link that a stranger can open and use to get the core benefit.

**Why it matters:** This week you ship something rough that a stranger can use, and it is fine, even expected, for parts of it to be manual.

**Watch and read**

- [Tips For Technical Startup Founders](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rP7bpYsfa6Q) (Diana Hu, Y Combinator). Useful even if you do not code, for how to move fast on a first version.
- [Do Things that Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) (Paul Graham). Read it before you decide what to automate and what to do by hand.

**Steps**

1. Write the single flow a user must complete to get the main benefit.
2. Build only that flow, using the fastest method available to you.
3. Test it with three people while you watch, and fix what breaks.
4. Record a short walkthrough and write down your success measure.

**MVP plan template**

```
The one flow (start to finish):
1.
2.
3.

How I will build it:
[Concierge, no-code, simple code]

What I am skipping on purpose:
-

Success measure:
[What would tell me it worked]

Test results (3 people):
[Where they got stuck]
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] A working link is provided that a stranger can open and use
- [ ] Describes the one core flow that delivers the main benefit
- [ ] Explains how the MVP was built, including any manual steps
- [ ] Reports testing with at least three people and what broke
- [ ] Scope is limited to the core flow and matches the features chosen in week 3

**Common mistakes**

- Building accounts, dashboards and settings before anyone has used the core flow.
- Waiting to share until it feels finished.
- Not testing it with anyone before sending it to real users.

## Week 6: Get first users

Ten real people using what you built, and what they told you.

**Hand in:** Ten real users who completed the core flow, and what you learned from them.

**Why it matters:** Ten real users who completed your flow is the single strongest piece of evidence you will have, and getting them takes personal effort.

**Watch and read**

- [How to Get Your First Customers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hyYCn_kAngI) (Gustaf Alströmer, Y Combinator). Write a list of ten names and a message for each before you finish.

**Steps**

1. Write down your definition of a user before you start.
2. Invite people personally from your week 2 interviews, your signups and the right communities.
3. Watch each user try the core flow and write down what they say.
4. Count how many came back, and decide the one change you will make next.

**User log template**

```
Definition of a user:
[What someone must do to count]

For each user:
Name or role:
How I found them:
Did they complete the flow:
What they said, in their words:
Did they come back:

Totals:
Users:
Came back:

The one change I will make next:
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] States a clear definition of a user based on completing the core flow
- [ ] Reports at least 10 users who match that definition
- [ ] Explains how the users were found, with real outreach rather than only friends
- [ ] Includes real feedback from users, ideally in their own words
- [ ] Reports how many users came back and names one concrete change planned

**Common mistakes**

- Counting signups or page visits as users.
- Only asking friends who will say yes to be polite.
- Summarising feedback in your own words and losing the actual quotes.

## Week 7: Pitch deck

Tell the story of your startup in ten slides and three minutes.

**Hand in:** An 8 to 10 slide deck and a 3-minute recorded pitch.

**Why it matters:** You now have real evidence, so this week is about telling the story clearly in ten slides and three minutes.

**Watch and read**

- [How to Pitch Your Startup](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6q-how-to-pitch-your-startup) (Kevin Hale, Y Combinator). Structure your deck and script around it.
- [Startup Business Models and Pricing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWZbWzAyHAE) (Aaron Epstein, Y Combinator). Helps with the business model slide.

**Steps**

1. Draft the ten slides using the structure and your real numbers from weeks 2 to 6.
2. Write a 400-word script and trim it to fit three minutes.
3. Pitch to your pod, listen to what they remember, and revise.
4. Record the final three-minute pitch and share both links.

**Three-minute pitch script template**

```
Opening (20 sec):
[The problem, in a way anyone understands]

What I built (30 sec):

Proof (70 sec):
[Conversations, users, return numbers, one strong quote]

What I learned (20 sec):

Why me and why now (20 sec):

The ask (20 sec):
[One clear, specific request]
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] A deck link is provided that anyone with the link can open
- [ ] The deck covers problem, solution, product, traction, team and an ask, in 8 to 10 slides
- [ ] A recorded pitch link is provided and is intended to run about three minutes
- [ ] Traction in the deck uses real numbers from the earlier milestones
- [ ] The ask is specific rather than a general request for support

**Common mistakes**

- Spending the pitch on market size and almost nothing on evidence.
- A vague ask like 'we are looking for support'.
- Reading the slides aloud instead of telling the story.

## Week 8: Final submission

Update your numbers, polish your pitch, and tell investors who you are.

**Hand in:** Updated traction numbers, your final pitch, and a one-page investor brief.

**Why it matters:** This is your final submission, and it helps to understand how investors think about early stage companies before you write your brief.

**Watch and read**

- [How Startup Fundraising Works](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zBUhQPPS9AY) (Brad Flora, Y Combinator). Use it to sharpen the ask in your brief.

**Steps**

1. Update every traction number to today and note what changed since week 6.
2. Polish your deck and re-record your pitch if you can make it clearly better.
3. Write your one-page brief using the template.
4. Submit your startup name, one-liner, brief, links and ask.

**One-page investor brief template**

```
Startup name:
One-liner:

Who it is for and the problem:

Evidence (conversations and what you learned):

Traction (numbers as of today):

What changed since week 1:

What I will do in the next 90 days:

What I want from investors and mentors:
```

**Pass checklist**

- [ ] Provides a startup name and a clear one-line description
- [ ] The brief explains who it is for, the problem and the evidence
- [ ] Traction numbers are updated and stated honestly
- [ ] States what the founder plans to do next
- [ ] Includes working links to the final deck and the final pitch, and a specific ask

**Common mistakes**

- Rounding numbers up or leaving out the ones that went down.
- A brief that describes the product but gives no evidence.
- Submitting links that nobody outside your team can open.

## Choosing the Demo Day founders

Score each of four areas from 0 to 25, on what the founder built and learned, never on college or grades.

- **Demand:** Evidence that real people want this: conversations, signups, returning users.
- **Execution:** Quality of what was built and shipped, and how fast.
- **Pitch:** Clarity of the idea, the brief and the ask.
- **Momentum:** Progress across the eight weeks, not just the final state.

## Running it yourself

1. **Start a batch on a fixed date each month.** Everyone in a batch starts together. Week 1 opens on the start date and a new week opens every seven days.
2. **Put founders in pods of four to five.** Mix colleges where you can. Pods give accountability: each founder can see where the others are.
3. **Review against the written checklist.** A week is passed only when every line on its checklist is met. Give specific feedback and allow resubmission.
4. **Check the work that cannot be checked in writing.** Sample passed weeks by hand, open the links, and have pod members confirm each other's landing pages, MVPs and users.
5. **Rank at the end, then interview.** Score everyone who passed weeks 1 to 7 on the four-part rubric. Interview the top 25 to 30 and ask for a live demo.
6. **Hold a Demo Day.** Up to 20 founders pitch for three minutes each to invited investors and mentors, followed by questions. Send investors a one-page brief per founder beforehand.
