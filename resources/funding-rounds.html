Notice board · The whole guide in three lines

Every round is a bet on something different.

Investors at each stage are buying a different kind of proof. Know which one you're selling.

EARLY

Early rounds back the people.

Bootstrapping to pre-seed. There's little data, so investors judge you.

MIDDLE

Middle rounds back the proof.

Seed and Series A. People use it, and it's starting to grow.

LATE

Later rounds back the scale.

Series B to IPO. Money goes into new cities, products and markets.

The full ladder · Rough sizes for Indian startups

Every funding round, in order

Not every company climbs every rung. Zerodha became a unicorn without raising a single round.

  1. 00

    Bootstrapping

    You

    Building with your own savings or early revenue. You keep all the equity.

    Your money
  2. 01

    Friends & family

    People who trust you

    A first small cheque from people who know you, before there's much to show.

    A few lakh
  3. 02

    Pre-seed

    Angels, pre-seed funds, accelerators

    Idea, team and maybe a prototype. Investors bet on the founders. Most student founders are here.
    Launchpad is built for this rung

    $150K–$500K≈ ₹1.25–4 Cr
  4. 03

    Seed

    Seed funds, micro-VCs, syndicates

    A live product with early users and first revenue. Investors bet that people want it.

    ~$1.3M avg≈ ₹12 Cr · H1 2026
  5. 04

    Series A

    Venture capital firms

    Repeatable revenue and steady growth. Investors bet that it can scale.

    $5M–$15M
  6. 05

    Series B

    Larger VCs, growth funds

    A proven model growing fast. Money goes into new cities, customers and teams.

    ~$15M–$50M
  7. 06

    Series C

    Growth funds, private equity

    Market leadership. Money for new products, new markets and acquisitions.

    ~$50M+
  8. 07

    Series D and beyond

    Late-stage and crossover funds

    A large company, often preparing to go public or buying more time to get there.

    $100M+
  9. 08

    Pre-IPO

    Late-stage funds, wealthy investors

    The last private round, a few months before listing.

    Varies
  10. 09

    IPO

    The public

    The company lists on the NSE or BSE and anyone can buy shares.

    Varies widely

Pre-seed and Series A ranges: Indian deal trackers via The Founder Nation (Jul 2026). Seed average: Tracxn India Tech H1 2026 via StartupFeed. Series B onwards: broad rules of thumb. Rupee figures are rough conversions.

Not every raise is a new letter

Special rounds you'll hear about

Between rounds

Bridge round

A small raise to buy time until the next big round.

Same round, more money

Extension (A+, B+)

Extra money added to the last round, usually on the same terms.

Warning sign

Down round

Raising at a lower valuation than last time.

Good sign

Up round

Raising at a higher valuation than last time.

A loan, not equity

Venture debt

Loans to funded startups. Little or no equity, but you pay it back.

Free money

Grants

Non-dilutive money from schemes like NIDHI-PRAYAS. Slow, with conditions.

Many small cheques

Crowdfunding

Lots of people put in small amounts. Limited by regulation in India.

No new money

Secondary sale

Existing shareholders sell to new investors. The company gets nothing.

Words you'll hear in every pitch meeting

The funding dictionary

Valuation
What investors agree your company is worth.
Pre-money / post-money
Your valuation before the new money comes in, and after.
Dilution
How much your ownership shrinks when new shares go to investors.
Cap table
The list of who owns what percentage of your company.
iSAFE
An India-adapted SAFE: money now, shares later at the next priced round.
CCPS
Compulsorily convertible preference shares, the usual way investors hold Indian startup shares.
Term sheet
A short document with the key deal terms before the legal paperwork.
Due diligence
The investor checking your numbers and legal setup before investing.
Burn rate
How much money you spend each month.
Runway
How many months you can last before the money runs out.
Unicorn
A private startup valued at $1 billion or more.
Exit
When investors get their money out, through an IPO or an acquisition.
